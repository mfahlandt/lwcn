## 📰 This Week in Cloud Native

The cloud native ecosystem saw a week of steady development, with numerous open-source projects releasing patch updates. A notable milestone was the **CNCF Technical Oversight Committee's decision to accept Meshery as an incubating project**, recognizing its role in managing cloud native infrastructure and service meshes. Looking ahead, the community is preparing for KubeCon + CloudNativeCon North America 2026, with announcements for co-located events such as Platform Engineering Day, CiliumCon, BackstageCon, and Kubernetes on Edge Day, indicating a broad range of specialized topics and active community engagement.

In **Kubernetes and Infrastructure**, significant technical discussions focused on the ongoing shift to cgroup v2. The Kubernetes blog published a detailed article explaining the implications of this transition, which impacts how resources like CPU and memory are managed for containers. Another article explored "Node Swap" as a strategy for scaling Kubernetes workloads, particularly relevant for memory-intensive applications such as emergent AI workloads. New open-source tools also emerged, including `K10s`, a clickable Kubernetes TUI, and `KubeNPU`, which provides per-pod NPU/GPU usage monitoring on Kubernetes via eBPF.

**Security updates** were released across several projects, including Envoy and Dapr, addressing vulnerabilities such as crashes on malformed requests and issues with spoofed caller identities or access control bypasses. Harbor also released patch versions that include a breaking change, blocking webhooks to internal addresses by default to mitigate potential security risks. Beyond project releases, OpenSSH 10.6 introduced deliberate breaking changes by disabling compression and usernames in certain contexts to enhance security. The broader discussion around security extended to regulatory compliance, with a CNCF blog post exploring how Kubernetes platform teams address requirements like NIS2 and DORA. The application of AI in security was also discussed, including the use of AI for identifying potential bugs in open-source code.

The **evolving role of AI agents** continued to be a prominent theme, with discussions spanning their security, operational implications, and impact on open-source development. Articles explored the risks of granting AI agents excessive permissions, advocating for models where agents propose system states rather than directly executing commands. Topics also included defining ownership for resources provisioned by AI agents, securing agent credentials, designing agent-friendly APIs, and adapting incident response strategies for AI-driven systems. The distinction between "open source" and "open weight" in AI terminology was also debated, highlighting the need for precise language in the rapidly developing AI landscape. Open-source projects like `AI SRE Arena` were introduced as benchmarks for AI SRE agents on Kubernetes, and `Genkit Dart 1.0` was announced for building agentic applications. Linus Torvalds also publicly stated his use of LLMs to assist in understanding and debugging code.
